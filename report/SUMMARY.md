# Pure Pursuit vs. Stanley for DV Autocross

**Verdict: Stanley.** Full argument, figures and derivations in [REPORT.md](REPORT.md).

## What was built

One Simulink model in which **only the steering law changes** — track, speed
reference, longitudinal PI, steering actuator, 100 Hz control rate and solver are
identical for both, so every measured difference belongs to the steering law.
MATLAB + Simulink only; both laws, the vehicle model, the track generator and the
KPIs are written from scratch, and the model is generated by script rather than
shipped as a binary.

The track is a closed filleted polygon: 212.6 m lap, 12 m minimum turning
diameter, 53.2 m longest straight, 3.5 m wide, closing to 0.23 mm — compliant with
Formula Student Rules 2026 D 8.1. Pure pursuit runs at the rear axle, Stanley at
the front axle, with gains chosen by grid search against one shared objective.

Verification: 21 analytical unit tests, including a closed-form check that each law
returns the Ackermann angle `atan(L/R)` on a circle — which caught a real defect,
the pure pursuit goal point being marched along arc length instead of found on the
chord. The Simulink model agrees with an independent RK4 reference loop to
4.4·10⁻¹¹ m over a lap.

## Results, nominal lap

| KPI | Pure Pursuit | Stanley |
|---|---|---|
| 1. lap time | **17.960 s** | 18.180 s |
| 2. max cross-track error | 0.902 m | **0.129 m** |
| 3. RMS cross-track error | 0.408 m | **0.055 m** |
| 4. RMS steering rate | **8.73 °/s** | 21.12 °/s |
| 5. cones Down or Out | 0 | 0 |
| effective time (D 10.1.7) | **17.960 s** | 18.180 s |
| FSG DV points (D 9.3.2) | **100.00** | 98.87 |

**A large tracking error is not automatically a fault here.** The reference is
the *centreline* — the midpoint between the cone boundaries — and the centreline
is not the fast way round a track. A racing line deliberately runs wide into a
corner and cuts to the apex, so it is shorter and faster than the centreline
precisely by *deviating* from it. Cross-track error measures fidelity to the
reference line, not the quality of the line being driven.

That is why pure pursuit laps faster while tracking seven times worse. Both
controllers receive the same speed reference at the same station, so lap time
reduces to distance driven: pure pursuit steers along a chord to a point ahead
on the line, which passes inside every arc, and drives 208.54 m against
Stanley's 210.17 m on a 212.64 m centreline. Held to Stanley's accuracy it laps
in 18.320 s — slower. Its advantage is corner cutting, not a better control law.

So in this study KPIs 2 and 3 are read as **margin** metrics rather than quality
metrics: what they really measure is how much room is left before the car
reaches the cones. That is exactly the quantity the verdict below turns on.

## The deciding experiment

The above is on a 3.5 m track; **D 8.1.1 guarantees only 3 m.** Clearance is the
gap to the cone line at the worst point of the lap:

| width | speed | Pure Pursuit | Stanley |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.50 m | ×1.00 | +0.25 m, 0 cones, **100.00 pts** | +1.02 m, 0 cones, 98.87 pts |
| 3.50 m | ×1.30 | +0.11 m, 0 cones, **100.00 pts** | +1.04 m, 0 cones, 99.09 pts |
| 3.00 m | ×1.00 | −0.00 m, 2 cones, 80.29 pts | +0.77 m, 0 cones, **100.00 pts** |
| 3.00 m | ×1.30 | −0.14 m, 6 cones, 50.53 pts | +0.79 m, 0 cones, **100.00 pts** |

Pure pursuit's 1.13-point lead exists only on a track wider than the rules
promise. At the guaranteed minimum it loses 20 to 50 points to cone penalties,
while Stanley never drops below 0.77 m of clearance or touches a cone at any width
or speed tested. Robustness to pose latency (0–100 ms) and position noise
(0–10 cm) is equivalent once gains are chosen outside the design envelope.

**Stanley is chosen** because its points do not depend on a generous track, and
0.77 m of margin against 0.25 m is room for what this model omits: SLAM drift, a
mis-detected cone, a wet patch. It costs 0.22 s a lap. Pure pursuit stays the
better answer where the steering actuator is the binding constraint, or where the
loop delay cannot be characterised.

## Judgement: neither law belongs on a high-speed car

Both are geometric laws on a kinematic bicycle model — no tyre model, no slip. For
an F1-class car, or any vehicle at the grip limit, that disqualifies **both**, and
a dynamic-model controller such as LQR or MPC with tyre stiffness is required.

Of the two, pure pursuit is the less suitable at speed despite winning on lap time
here: its lookahead grows with speed, so its corner cutting grows with speed, and
its entire advantage comes from deliberately running closer to the track limit.
When the cost of an unmodelled slip is leaving the track rather than brushing a
cone, spending margin to buy tenths is the wrong trade. Stanley — hold the line,
keep the margin — degrades more gracefully when the omitted physics starts to
matter.
